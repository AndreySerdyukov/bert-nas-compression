The αNAS paper is about searching over _properties_ of an architecture rather than its concrete
description - abstracting a network into the invariants that matter, mutating those, and
synthesising a concrete model back out. The implementation here keeps the evolutionary skeleton and
reduces the property to a single one: depth, encoded as the same twelve-bit mask.

## How it works

Start with a population of four: the all-ones mask plus three mutants of it. Score each, keep the
best two, refill the population by mutating them, repeat for three generations. Mutation flips each
bit with probability 0.3 and then repairs the result back into the 4-12 range.

The fitness adds a hard constraint to the soft trade-off:

```
reward = accuracy - 1e-8 * params,  rejected outright if accuracy < 0.75 or params > 1e8
```

## The seed that could never survive

The first candidate in generation 1 is the all-ones mask - the full twelve-layer model - and it is
rejected. Not because it scored badly; it scored 0.8979, the best accuracy in the entire run. It is
rejected because 109 483 778 parameters exceed the hard cap of 100 000 000.

That cap and that seed are incompatible by construction. The population is initialised with a
member that the constraint can never accept, which wastes a quarter of the first generation on an
architecture whose only possible outcome is rejection. It is visible as the crossed circle in the
chart, and it recurs every time the search is run.

## What it converged to

By generation 3 the population had settled on `[1, 2, 6, 11]` - four layers, 52 780 802 parameters,
0.8536 on the search's own split. That is a sensible-looking result: it keeps something early,
something in the middle and the last layer.

One number after it is not sensible. The final line of the notebook re-evaluates the winner and
reports 0.7679 - below the 0.75 floor the search itself would have rejected. The cause is in the
call: the validation loader is passed as both the training and the evaluation set. The published
checkpoint comes from a separate notebook that does not have this problem, so the model is fine; the
number printed at the end of the search is not.

## Evolution on twelve bits

Worth saying plainly: with twelve binary decisions, three generations and a population of four, an
evolutionary algorithm has very little room to be evolutionary. Twelve candidates were evaluated,
one of which was rejected on arrival and several of which were re-evaluations of survivors. What
this run demonstrates is that the machinery works, not that evolution beat the alternatives.
