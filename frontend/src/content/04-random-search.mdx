Random Search is the method that keeps embarrassing the others. Li and Talwalkar's 2019 paper is
mostly an argument that a properly tuned random baseline matches most published NAS results on the
benchmarks of the time, and that many reported gains were comparisons against a random search that
had been given less budget or worse hyperparameters.

So it belongs here twice: as a method, and as the control every other method has to beat.

## How it works

Draw `k` uniformly between 4 and 12, draw `k` layer indices without replacement, skip duplicates,
train the candidate for one epoch, score it. Repeat until the budget runs out. There is no state -
nothing learned from one draw informs the next - which is exactly what makes it the right floor.

The score is not accuracy alone. It trades quality against size explicitly:

```
score = alpha * accuracy + beta * (1 - log(params + 1) / log(max_params))
```

with `alpha = 0.3` and `beta = 0.7` as it was actually run. Those weights matter more than they
look: at that setting the size term dominates, so a smaller model wins unless a larger one is
substantially more accurate.

## Three draws

The budget was three candidates. Not thirty, not three hundred - three, on 2 800 training rows.

The first draw kept ten layers and scored 0.9157, far and away the best accuracy of the three. It
also has the best fitness of the three, at 0.2801. It is not what was chosen. The winner was the
third draw, a five-layer mask at 0.8429 and a fitness of 0.2759 - the best of the two five-layer
candidates, which is a defensible thing to want, but it is not what the fitness function was ranking.

There is a second discrepancy, and it is larger. The selection notebook and the tech report both
name `[0, 1, 6, 8, 10]` as the winner. The notebook that trained and uploaded the published
checkpoint uses `[0, 1, 5, 7, 9]`. Both keep five layers, so every table of parameter counts agrees
and nothing downstream noticed. The model on the Hub - the one this app serves and benchmarks - is
the second one.

## What three draws can support

Not much, and that is the honest reading. With three samples from a space of 3 797, the result is
approximately "a five-layer model at 91% exists", which is worth knowing and is not evidence about
search strategy. The paper this chapter is named after would say the interesting comparison is
against a simple rule - spread the layers evenly, keep the first five - trained the same way. That
comparison was never run here, which is why the rebuild runs it.
