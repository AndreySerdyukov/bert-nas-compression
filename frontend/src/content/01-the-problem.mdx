A BERT-base sentiment classifier holds 109 483 778 parameters. On disk that is 438 MB of float32.
It reads a film review and answers one of two things.

The mismatch is not subtle, and it is not specific to sentiment. `bert-base-uncased` was pretrained
to be good at everything language-shaped, and a task that needs one bit of output inherits the whole
apparatus: twelve encoder layers, twelve attention heads each, a 3072-wide feed-forward block in
every layer. The question this project asks is how much of that a single task actually needs, and
the answer turns out to be a good deal less than all of it.

## The three things that get traded

Compression is usually sold as one number - "2× smaller" - but three quantities move, and they do
not move together.

**Parameters** decide how much memory a copy of the model occupies, and therefore how many copies
fit on a machine. **FLOPs** decide how much arithmetic one prediction costs. **Accuracy** decides
whether any of it was worth doing. The first two are properties of the architecture and can be
computed exactly. The third has to be measured, and measuring it means training the model first.

That asymmetry runs through everything here. It is why an interactive layer picker can report a
parameter count the instant you click, and why it cannot report an accuracy without a minute of
compute. It is also why architecture search is expensive: every candidate you want to rank has to be
trained before you can rank it.

## The budget

The project set itself a limit: cut the model down without losing more than seven points of
accuracy. That number is arbitrary the way all such numbers are, but stating it up front is what
turns "make it smaller" into a question with an answer.

Four methods, four points below and to the left of the baseline, all inside the budget. Read at face
value the picture is a success: half the parameters for two and a half points, or a fourteenth of
the parameters for five.

Read more carefully it raises the question this rebuild exists to answer. Every one of those four
points sits below a baseline that was fine-tuned three separate times, by three people, to
93.3%, 93.7% and 94.1%. The spread between three runs of _the same architecture_ is 0.8 points. The
gap being celebrated is 2.5. Those numbers are close enough together that the comparison needs to be
made more carefully than "one of ours against one of theirs" - and that is before anything is said
about how the latencies were timed.
