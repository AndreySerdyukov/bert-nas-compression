Neural Architecture Search is the idea that the shape of a network is a thing you can optimise
rather than a thing you decide. Every method in this project, and essentially every method in the
literature, decomposes into the same three parts.

## Search space

The set of architectures you are willing to consider. This is a design decision and it is doing more
work than any other part: a search can only ever return something the space contains, so a space
that excludes the good answer guarantees failure no matter how clever the strategy is. Making the
space bigger makes the search harder and rarely makes it better.

## Search strategy

How you pick the next architecture to look at. Random sampling is a strategy. So is evolution, so is
Bayesian optimisation over a surrogate model, so is relaxing the discrete choice into a continuous
one and following the gradient. All four appear in this project, and they are what the four chapters
after this one are about.

## Performance estimation

How you find out whether the architecture you picked is any good - and this is where the cost is.

Estimating an architecture honestly means training it. A candidate here got one epoch, which took
between two and four minutes on a subsample. That is the unit of currency for the whole exercise: a
search's budget is measured in candidates, and a candidate is measured in training runs.

The consequence is worth sitting with. Even the small space these methods used holds thousands of
architectures, and looking at all of them is days of GPU time for a task where the full model
already works. So every NAS method is, structurally, a way of _not_ looking at most of the space -
and the interesting question about any of them is whether the architectures it chose to skip were
worth skipping.

## Where the estimate can go wrong

Because training every candidate to convergence is unaffordable, every method cheats somewhere:
fewer epochs, less data, shorter sequences, weight sharing. Those shortcuts are what make search
tractable and they are also its main failure mode, because a cheap estimate that does not correlate
with the real ranking turns the whole search into an expensive random draw.

That is not a hypothetical here. One of the four searches ran its estimates on 560 training rows at
128 tokens and got candidate accuracies between 0.58 and 0.69 - close enough to chance that
re-evaluating the same architecture twice moved it by 4.3 points, which is wider than the gap it was
trying to detect. Chapter 6 covers what that does to a Bayesian method that assumes its observations
mean something.
