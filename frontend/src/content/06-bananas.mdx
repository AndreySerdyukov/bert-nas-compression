BANANAS is the most interesting method here and the one whose run went worst, for reasons that have
nothing to do with the method.

The idea is Bayesian optimisation applied to architectures. Training a candidate is expensive, so
train a cheap _surrogate_ that predicts a candidate's score from its description, use the surrogate
to decide what to try next, and spend the expensive evaluation only on that. Every observation
improves the surrogate, so the search should get better at choosing as it goes.

## The four pieces

**A surrogate.** An ensemble of three small networks, 12 → 16 → 16 → 1, trained by mean squared
error on everything evaluated so far. An ensemble rather than one model because the spread between
its members is the uncertainty estimate, and Bayesian optimisation needs uncertainty as much as it
needs a prediction.

**An encoding.** The paper's central contribution is _path encoding_ - describing an architecture by
the set of input-to-output paths through its operation graph - and the report here says outright
that it does not transfer, because this search space is a layer mask and not a graph of operations.
The raw twelve bits are used instead. That is an honest substitution and it costs the method the
thing the paper was arguing for.

**An acquisition function.** Independent Thompson sampling: draw one value per candidate from the
ensemble's predicted distribution and take the argmax. Sampling rather than maximising the mean is
what makes the search explore.

**An acquisition optimiser.** Mutate the better half of what has been seen to generate the next batch
of proposals, then score all of them with the surrogate and evaluate the best.

## Why it could not work here

The dashed rule separates five initial random samples from five model-guided iterations. The problem
is on the vertical axis: every candidate scores between 0.58 and 0.69, on a task where the full model
reaches 0.93.

The searches were run on 560 training rows at a sequence length of 128 - roughly a fifth of the data
the other methods used, at a quarter of the sequence length. At that budget the model is barely above
chance, and the differences between architectures are buried in run-to-run noise.

The clearest evidence is in the log itself. Iterations 7 and 8 evaluate the **same configuration**,
`[0, 1, 6, 9]`, and get 0.6214 and 0.5786. A 4.3-point spread on identical inputs, when the entire
range the search is trying to rank spans about 11 points. The surrogate is being fit to values whose
noise is a third of their signal, and Thompson sampling over a surrogate fit to noise is a slow way
of sampling uniformly.

## The result, and what it means

The best configuration by fitness is `[0, 1, 6, 9]` - four layers - and when it was later trained
properly on the full 28 000 rows it reached 0.9031 on the test set. That is a genuinely good model.

It is worth being careful about what that shows. The architecture is fine. The claim that Bayesian
optimisation _found_ it is not supported by this run, because the observations the surrogate learned
from could not distinguish the candidates. A final print compounds this: the reported accuracy of the
winner, 0.6000, is a leaked loop variable from the last iteration rather than the 0.6214 the winning
configuration actually scored.
