The teaching feedback on the original project was short and correct: three people each fine-tuning
their own baseline and comparing against each other is not a measurement, and there are established
benchmarks for exactly this reason.

That single remark reorganises the whole project, because it identifies what was missing. Not
accuracy, not effort - a **control**.

## The comparison that was never run

Three NAS methods produced masks of four and five layers. Nobody trained a four-layer model with the
layers chosen by a rule - every third one, the first four, the last four - under the same protocol
and compared. Without that, "NAS found a good architecture" and "four-layer BERTs are fine on IMDB"
predict exactly the same observations, and nothing in the project separates them.

So the rebuild runs it. Evenly spaced, first-k and last-k masks at four and five layers; several
random masks per size, so the searched mask can be placed in a distribution rather than next to a
single point; DistilBERT, to ask what distillation buys at a comparable size; and TF-IDF with
logistic regression, to ask how much of this task needs a transformer at all. Every one of them
fine-tuned for one epoch on the same 28 000 rows at 512 tokens - the protocol the shipped Random
Search and BANANAS models got - and scored by the same harness on the same test set.

It is entirely possible that an evenly spaced mask matches the searched one. If it does, that is the
result and it gets published as the result. A control is only worth running if you are willing to
lose to it.

## It lost

All eighteen ran, and the answer depends on the depth - which is itself the first thing worth
knowing, because a single verdict would have to pick one depth to believe.

At four layers the search beat the evenly spaced mask: AlphaNAS at 0.9027 and BANANAS at 0.9031
against 0.8959. At five layers it did not: Random Search at 0.9077 against 0.9080, a gap of four
reviews out of fifteen thousand.

The evenly spaced mask then turned out to be one particular way of being naive rather than a floor
under all of them. Keeping the bottom k layers - `range(k)`, no rule to speak of - beats the search
at both depths, 0.9062 against 0.9031 and 0.9114 against 0.9077. Which end of the stack the layers
come from is worth 2.63 points at both depths, five times what a fifth layer buys, and the searched
masks used that lever half way.

Placed against five random masks of its own depth, each drawn with a seed and trained identically,
the four-layer results sit **below the median** - AlphaNAS at the 20th percentile of the five,
BANANAS at the 40th. Random Search clears its own median, by 0.10 points. The spread of five random
masks at four layers is 1.25 points, thirty times the 0.04 that separates AlphaNAS from BANANAS and
that the original tables were read as a ranking.

The two controls that are not masks are the least comfortable. TF-IDF with logistic regression, ten
seconds of CPU and no transformer at all, scores 0.9141 - above all four searched architectures.
DistilBERT scores 0.9295 at 66 955 010 parameters, where a six-layer mask would be 66 956 546: the
same size to within 1 536 parameters, giving up 0.35 points to full BERT where the best searched
model gives up 2.53. One caveat belongs to it and runs in the search's favour - distillation
pre-training is compute the masked BERTs never received, so this is an honest comparison for an
engineer choosing a model and not one between two compression algorithms at equal cost.

The careful way to state all of this is not that naive rules win. A Wilson interval on 15 000 rows is
about ±0.46 points, and everything from 0.9003 to 0.9114 is one flat band inside it. What the
measurement supports is narrower and worse: **the result of the search is indistinguishable from a
random mask of the same depth**, and the search cost days of compute where the random mask costs one
line.

## One baseline, not three

The three fine-tunes of the same architecture span 0.8 points. The compression gap being celebrated
is 2.5. Publishing three baselines and comparing against whichever suits makes that spread invisible;
publishing one, and reporting the spread separately as what it is - run-to-run variance - keeps it in
view.

## Estimation budgets that could rank things

Every search here spent its budget on more candidates rather than on better estimates, and two of
them ended up ranking noise. Given the same total compute, fewer candidates evaluated on enough data
to separate them would have produced a search whose observations meant something. That is the
trade-off performance estimation always presents, and this project got it backwards.

## Measure once, in one place

Four tables existed because four things measured. One harness, one committed artifact, and a README
generated from it - which is what this repository does now, with CI failing if the prose and the JSON
disagree.

## What still stands

Worth ending here rather than on the list of problems. Four NAS strategies were implemented from
scratch and they work. The layer-masking machinery is correct, the parameter arithmetic is exact, the
four-layer models are real models that really do reach 90% while running two to three times faster,
and the checkpoints have been public and loadable for over a year - which is more than most course
projects can say.

What the rebuild adds is not better models. It is knowing what they are worth.
