This chapter exists because the rebuild had to choose which numbers to publish, and choosing meant
reading every source. What follows is what that turned up. Each item is a property of a script or a
method, never of a person - the work was a three-person student project done under a deadline, and
most of these are the kind of thing that survives precisely because nobody had a reason to look
twice.

## There are four result tables and they disagree

The same seven models are reported in the eval notebooks, in the head-to-head notebook, in four tech
reports and in the final presentation. Almost no two agree.

Only `NAS_Results.ipynb` scores every model on one common test set with one harness, so that is the
table this project treats as the original's answer - and the one the new measurements are compared
against. The tech-report figures (91.25, 91.37, 90.5, 89.79, 93.99) are superseded rather than
merged.

Two disagreements are worth naming. Alina's baseline reports 0.9188 in its own notebook and 0.9367
when the uploaded checkpoint is re-scored: the largest single gap in the project, and evidence the
two are different training runs. BANANAS is the only method whose eval notebook and the shared
benchmark agree exactly - and even it disagrees with its own tech report, which reports 90.5% where
the notebook prints 90.31%.

## Random Search reports one architecture and ships another

The selection notebook and the tech report both name `[0, 1, 6, 8, 10]`. The eval notebook, which
trained and uploaded the checkpoint, sets `[0, 1, 5, 7, 9]`. Both keep five layers, so the parameter
count is identical in every table and nothing downstream noticed.

The model on the Hub is the second one. This app reports it, and a unit test pins the distinction so
it cannot be quietly reconciled later.

## AlphaNAS's final re-evaluation trains on its own evaluation set

The last call passes `val_loader` as both the training and the evaluation loader, and reports 0.7679
where the search had measured 0.8536 for the same mask. The published checkpoint comes from a
different notebook and is unaffected; the number at the end of the selection notebook is not a
measurement of anything.

## BANANAS searched at a budget that could not rank anything

560 training rows at 128 tokens, against 2 800 and 5 600 at 512 for the others. Candidate accuracies
land between 0.58 and 0.69, and the same configuration evaluated twice scores 0.6214 and 0.5786. The
final print reports the last iteration's accuracy rather than the winner's.

## The search budgets differ by a factor of fifty

Three candidates on 5 000 rows, twelve on 10 000, ten on 1 000, fifteen epochs on 49 582. No ranking
of "which NAS method is better" survives that, and this project does not make one. What it does
instead is add the controls that make a different, answerable question available: did searching beat
a simple rule?

## AdaBERT is not a compressed transformer

Covered in chapter 7. Every selected operation was parameter-free, so the shipped model is a bag of
embeddings. The checkpoint size confirms it to the byte.

## The latency figures measure something other than their label

Covered in chapter 8. Throughput reported as latency, no warm-up, no synchronisation, a mean over
one pass.

## The split overlaps the official IMDB partition

The Kaggle CSV is the standard corpus - 25 000 official train rows and 25 000 official test rows -
and the notebooks reshuffle all 50 000 with one seed. The resulting test set therefore contains rows
from the official training half, and the models saw rows from the official test half.

These checkpoints cannot be compared against published IMDB results. It is the reason every external
reference in this project is fine-tuned and measured here, on this same split, rather than quoted.

## Nothing runs outside Colab

Hardcoded Drive paths, no requirements file, no package structure, no seeding beyond the split.
Reproducing any of it meant having the original author's Google Drive - which is what this repository
is, in the end, an answer to.
